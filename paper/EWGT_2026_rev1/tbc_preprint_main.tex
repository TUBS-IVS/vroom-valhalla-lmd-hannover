\begin{abstract}
Time-based consolidation (TBC) lets logistics service providers delay suitable parcels for batched delivery, trading customer waiting time against operational savings. While the principle is well known, its cost-service balance, its spatial signature, and its fleet-side benefits across heterogeneous carriers remain insufficiently quantified. We optimize TBC schedules on the Hanover Region case ($312$ provider--postal-code cells formed by seven LSPs over $48$ postal-code areas, $1.26$~million weekly parcels) using a machine-learning surrogate that couples Daganzo's continuum approximation with a LightGBM residual ($2.95\%$ mean absolute percentage error, MAPE, under postal-code-grouped cross-validation), embedded in a coordinate-descent search over a service-penalty $P$ and a willingness-to-wait share $\theta$. Every priced tour --- in the daily baseline as in every consolidation scenario --- obeys one universal minimum/maximum rule, so no scenario may pool sparse deliveries in a way the baseline cannot. We report two cost lenses: a \emph{routing} lens that charges the full daily vehicle cost, and an \emph{operator} lens that charges only variable cost below each depot's weekly fleet peak. Under the routing lens, savings reach $22.6\%$ at the cost-optimal extreme; under the operator lens the same routing-optimal plan is $8.4\%$ \emph{worse} than daily delivery, because two-day patterns multiply depot peaks. A second-stage operator-cost polish turns this into $24.3\%$ operator saving at a $16.9\%$ lower summed depot peak. At full adoption the operator-polished plan's saving falls with the service penalty in both lenses, so on cost alone each lens, read on its own plan, prefers $P = 0$, by $3.9$~pp in the routing lens and $1.7$~pp in the operator lens. Once waiting is priced we nonetheless recommend $P = 0.25$~\euro\ per parcel-day: it halves the additional wait for $1.7$~pp of operator saving, and once a flat $0.50$~\euro\ discount per delayed parcel is paid out it is the best point on the grid in the operator lens, ahead of $P = 0.5$ by $0.8$~pp, with the routing lens rating the two within $0.01$~pp. In the routing lens three carrier classes emerge with distinct sweet spots in $P \in [0.25, 0.75]$~\euro\ per parcel-day, but the ordering itself depends on the lens and one carrier leaves the band under the operator lens; at full adoption and under the operator-polished plan the Monday--Saturday fleet coefficient of variation drops by $51$--$84\%$ across that range. Consolidation pays in the operator lens where a depot can rotate delivery days across several of its cells, whereas in the one multi-depot network of the case study depots serving a single cell revert to near-daily delivery and save kilometres but no fleet. Re-routing $7{,}610$ instances of the reported schedules and of the daily baseline with the solver shows that the surrogate over-prices every instance class, and the thin daily-baseline tours ($+5.4\%$) more than the consolidated ones ($+2.4\%$), so the predicted savings are an upper bound: the realized figures are $1.3$ to $2.5$~pp lower, $22.1\%$ of operator saving at $(P, \theta) = (0, 1)$ and $20.6\%$ of routing saving for the routing-optimal plan, while every sign and ordering is unchanged.

\section{Introduction and Related Work}
\label{intro}

In the European Union, $78\%$ of internet users purchased goods or services online in 2025 \citep{Eurostat2025}, illustrating how deeply parcel delivery is embedded in everyday consumption. Each online order creates a physical delivery task in the final segment of the parcel supply chain, commonly referred to as last-mile delivery (LMD), which is among the most expensive, inefficient, and environmentally burdensome parts of the supply chain \citep{Olsson2019}. For logistics service providers (LSPs), this intensifies existing pressures: LMD operations are already characterized by low productivity, restrictive delivery time windows, complex urban operating conditions, and high cost shares \citep{BACHOFNER2022100020}, reinforced by climate change, rapid urbanization, demographic change, and shifting demand patterns \citep{logistics2040025}.

At the same time, e-commerce has made delivery speed a visible part of the customer-facing service promise, so LSPs are expected to provide faster, more personalized, and more reliable services while prices and margins remain constrained \citep{Vivaldini}. However, not all customers prioritize the fastest possible delivery: \citet{Nguyen19} distinguish price-oriented, convenience-oriented, and value-for-money-oriented segments, indicating that some customers may accept slower delivery if it lowers cost. This heterogeneity makes delivery timing a potential operational control variable. Temporarily storing eligible parcels and dispatching them jointly can reduce costs compared with direct delivery \citep{PENG2023109044}, and a some-day delivery option can reduce cost and vehicle requirements when customers accept a latest delivery day \citep{Voigt2025SomeDayDelivery}; sustainability benefits, however, arise only if the temporal flexibility translates into better routing, lower fleet requirements, or reduced travel distances \citep{Zeelenberg2026WillingnessToWait}. Time-based consolidation (TBC) packages this trade-off into a delivery concept in which eligible customers accept a longer delivery window, for example in exchange for a lower price \citep{Naeimian2026TemporalConsolidation}, so that eligible shipments are temporarily held and grouped into batched deliveries within a predefined service promise. TBC therefore moves away from a uniform immediacy logic toward a differentiated service design in which customers retain choice while LSPs gain temporal flexibility.

This paper investigates TBC as an operational response to the tension between customer expectations, LSP constraints, and sustainability objectives. From a planning perspective, the core task is to assign eligible parcels to delivery days at the postal-code-area level such that delivery density increases, additional waiting time remains limited, and weekday demand peaks or uneven fleet use are avoided. Because each schedule induces a different demand pattern and thus different delivery routes, direct evaluation would require solving many Vehicle Routing Problems, which is computationally infeasible given the VRP's NP-hardness \citep{LenstraRinnooyKan1981}. We therefore propose an ML-based surrogate-optimization framework for identifying cost-efficient TBC schedules in a large-scale case study of the Hanover Region in Germany. The framework combines a postal-code-area scheduling model with a routing-cost surrogate trained on VRP solutions; building on ML-supported combinatorial optimization \citep{BENGIO2021405} and data-driven extensions of analytical LMD route-distance approximations \citep{Figliozzi08, KIRUBAKARAN2026101720}, it enables efficient schedule search across adoption scenarios. We make four contributions: a hybrid Daganzo--LightGBM routing-cost surrogate that couples an interpretable continuum backbone with a learned residual and is applied only inside a measured validity domain; a universal minimum/maximum tour rule that makes the daily baseline and every consolidation scenario structurally comparable; a scalable optimization of spatially and provider-differentiated TBC delivery-day patterns under two explicit cost lenses, per-day routing cost and weekly operator cost with staffed depot peaks; and empirical evidence on where TBC pays off, and on how the choice of lens changes both the recommended operating point and the ordering of carriers. Routing and demand simulation enter only as label generation and out-of-sample evaluation, so the framework is a surrogate-assisted delivery-schedule optimization rather than a simulation study.

\section{Methodology}
\label{methods}

Our framework used weekly parcel-demand patterns generated with our own HAGRID demand model \citep{BIENZEISLER2026104682}, covering seven LSPs in the Hanover Region (about $1.26$~million parcels and $1.90$~M\euro\ of baseline operating cost per week).
% src: tab_grid_full_v2.csv, row penalty=0 share_willing=0, routing_cost_plan1_eur = 1,898,091
As \autoref{fig:input_data} shows, demand varies strongly between dense urban and sparse rural postal-code areas, peaks midweek, and is lowest on Saturday. To make repeated routing evaluations tractable, we decomposed the study area into postal-code-level routing subproblems while preserving the demand, spatial, and depot-access characteristics relevant for delivery cost. The pipeline structure is summarized in \autoref{fig:framework}.\footnote{The implementation of the proposed framework is available at: \url{https://github.com/TUBS-IVS/vroom-valhalla-lmd-hannover}}


The unit of analysis is a \emph{cell}, defined as an LSP--postal-code-area pair assigned to the provider's serving hub and described by daily demand, area size, stop structure, and depot-access distance. The case study comprises $312$ such cells over $48$ postal-code areas, each area served by up to seven LSPs; all distributional statements below are per cell unless stated otherwise. The decision variable is the weekly \emph{delivery pattern}, i.e., the service weekdays for that cell; parcels from non-service days are held until the next scheduled service day. LSP operating costs are route-dependent. Following open-data-based LMD modeling \citep{HORL2025104893}, we generated routing-cost labels with VROOM \citep{vroom_v1.14} based on Valhalla-derived travel times and distances on OpenStreetMap road geometry.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/valhalla/valhalla}} Recurrent congestion was approximated by applying an empirically derived correction factor to free-flow travel times. The current daily-delivery operation served as the baseline for all consolidation scenarios.

\subsection{Cost approximation and machine-learning surrogate}
\label{sec:cost_model}

Solving a full VRP for every candidate schedule is computationally too costly, so we approximated operational cost with a continuous model based on Daganzo's distance formulation \citep{Daganzo84} and corrected it with a data-driven term. For area $z$ on day $d$, route distance and cost are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:daganzo}
\hat{D}_{z,d} = m_{z,d}\!\left( 2 r_z + \beta\sqrt{\tfrac{n_{z,d}}{m_{z,d}}\, A_z} \right), \qquad
\hat{C}_{z,d} = c_f\, m_{z,d} + c_d\, \hat{D}_{z,d},
\end{equation}
with $m_{z,d}=\lceil p_{z,d}/Q\rceil$ tours (parcel volume $p_{z,d}$, vehicle capacity $Q = 230$), average depot distance $r_z$, $n_{z,d}$ stops over area $A_z$, and network/shape factor $\beta$: each of the $m_{z,d}$ tours travels to the area and back ($2r_z$) and serves its $n_{z,d}/m_{z,d}$ stops across the service area. The rate $c_d$ prices distance and $c_f$ is the fixed daily cost per vehicle. Consolidation lowers cost by raising delivery density while the capacity term penalizes over-consolidation.

Such approximations reproduce the first-order scaling but are point-inaccurate, as they abstract from the road network, depot position, and demand clustering, normally requiring manual recalibration \citep{Figliozzi08}. Instead, we used Equation~\eqref{eq:daganzo} as a physically interpretable \emph{backbone} and learned its deviation from solver cost,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:surrogate}
\tilde{C}_{z,d} = \alpha\,\hat{C}_{z,d} + g(\mathbf{x}_{z,d}),
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is a global calibration factor and the structured residual $g(\cdot)$ is a LightGBM ensemble \citep{NIPS20176449f44a}, chosen because gradient-boosted trees handle tabular nonlinearities and feature interactions well, and expose feature importance for interpretation \citep{10.5555/3600270.3600307}. The scalar $\alpha$ was calibrated once as the median ratio of solver cost to the raw Daganzo prediction across the training pool, and $g(\cdot)$ then learned the structured remainder.

The quantity $\tilde{C}$ predicts is fixed by what the solver labels contain, and it is worth stating exactly. Every label --- and therefore every predicted, baseline, and validated cost reported in this paper --- follows the effective cost model $189.15$~\euro\ per vehicle and day, $0.3864$~\euro\ per kilometre, and $36$~\euro\ per route-hour, the last because VROOM's default per-hour rate was active in all labels; the three terms contribute $72.2$, $6.0$, and $21.7\%$ of label cost. The Daganzo backbone in Equation~\eqref{eq:daganzo} carries only the vehicle and distance terms, so $\alpha$ and the learned residual absorb the route-time component. The driver's wage is part of $c_f$, but the route-time charge is separate and additional, and is not contained in it. The consequence is that driver labour is charged twice: once inside $c_f$ and once through the route-time term. Baseline, scenarios, prediction, and out-of-sample validation all carry that term, so the comparison is internally consistent and the \emph{relative} savings we report are unaffected --- route time is dominated by per-parcel service time, which barely moves between daily and batched operation, so a near-constant term sits in numerator and denominator alike and damps rather than inflates the percentages. Absolute cost \emph{levels}, by contrast, contain it: about a fifth of every level reported here is this double-counted term (Supplementary Section~S2).

The feature vector $\mathbf{x}_{z,d}$ comprised 25 demand and geometry descriptors derived ex ante, following \citet{Akkerman2025} (parcel volume, stops, service-area size, depot distance, parcels per stop, the load factor $p_{z,d}/Q$, and per-stop demand statistics), with VROOM/Valhalla route costs as targets. To cover the consolidation cost range rather than only baseline operations, we augmented the pool with synthetic cell-day samples obtained by demand scaling, stop dropout, coordinate jitter, and multi-day aggregation, the last reproducing the elevated per-day volumes of batched delivery. Generalization to both sparse rural and dense urban areas was assessed by group $k$-fold cross-validation with folds grouped by postal-code area.

\subsection{Tour geometry and the validity domain of the surrogate}
\label{sec:tour_rule}

A surrogate may only be asked to price instances of the family it was trained on. Our training pool aggregates demand in \emph{time} --- up to three days of one postal-code area --- but never in \emph{space}: it contains no multi-area instance. We therefore constrain every tour the model prices, in the daily baseline exactly as in every consolidation scenario, by one universal rule. A tour carries at least $230$ parcels, one vehicle load, below which the out-of-fold error of the surrogate more than doubles, to $7.1\%$; at most $556$ stops, the $99$th percentile of the training pool; at most $159$~km$^2$ of service area, above which only $6.4\%$ of training rows lie and the surrogate's bias drifts negative, averaging $-4.3\%$ over the $159$--$358$~km$^2$ range; and its members' convex hull may not exceed $1.22$ times their summed area, the largest ratio observed in the training pool. A cell that reaches the parcel minimum on its own is always served alone. Cells below it are pooled with neighbouring cells of the same provider served by the same depot until the rule is met, greedily by proximity and subject to the three caps; the pooling is recomputed for every weekday and applies to the baseline as well.

Two properties matter for the interpretation of the results. First, the rule is \emph{scenario-blind} by construction: no code path lets a consolidation scenario pool the deliveries of sparse cells while the daily baseline pays a separate tour per cell and day. That asymmetry was present in the submitted version and inflated the savings reported at partial adoption; it is removed here. Second, non-consolidated parcels are not handled by a separate mechanism: on a day on which a cell runs no batched tour, the parcels of recipients who did not opt in form an ordinary instance of that cell and enter the same partition under the same rule.

Pooled instances lie outside the single-area training family, so they are priced by a dedicated \emph{bundle head}: the same $\alpha$-calibrated Daganzo backbone with a LightGBM residual, trained on $877$ solver-routed pooled tours and validated out-of-fold with folds grouped by member set. The head prices a pooled tour only in bins in which it is certified --- at least $6$ labels and an absolute out-of-fold bias of at most $5\%$ --- which is $21$ of $235$ bins, with $2.85\%$ out-of-fold MAPE ($2.71\%$ occurrence-weighted) and $1.35\%$ absolute bias; pool-wide, that is including the uncertified bins, the head reaches $5.37\%$ MAPE at $+0.57\%$ bias. Certification covered $44.0\%$ of the pooled instances expected before the run; in the optimized grid it priced $53.9\%$ of the pooled tours that actually occurred, and because the fallback keeps the largest ones, $27.2\%$ of the pooled euro ($68.9$ and $53.0\%$ at full adoption). These three rates are not interchangeable and we report them separately. Outside its certified support the head refuses to price and the pooled tour is charged as the sum of its members' single-cell prices, a deliberately conservative fallback that is counted and reported per operating point.






\subsection{Two cost lenses}
\label{sec:lenses}

A weekly schedule is not evaluated the same way by a route planner and by a depot manager, so we report every result under two lenses. Let $i$ index the priced instances of a solution --- single-cell tours and pooled tours alike --- with surrogate cost $\tilde{C}_i$ and vehicle requirement $v_i = \lceil p_i / Q \rceil$, and let $v_{h,d}$ be the vehicles a depot $h$ needs on weekday $d$. The \emph{routing lens} is the accounting of the submitted version: every vehicle-day is charged in full,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lens_routing}
C^{\mathrm{route}} = \sum_i \tilde{C}_i .
\end{equation}
The \emph{operator lens} instead reflects weekly staffing. Drivers are employed and vans are owned for the week, so a depot pays its fixed bill for the peak day and only the variable part of every other vehicle-day,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lens_operator}
C^{\mathrm{op}} = \underbrace{\sum_i \bigl( \tilde{C}_i - c_f\, v_i \bigr)}_{\text{variable (distance and time)}} \; + \; \underbrace{6\, c_f \sum_h \max_d v_{h,d}}_{\text{weekly fixed cost of the peak fleet}},
\end{equation}
with $c_f = 189.15$~\euro, so a peak vehicle costs $6 c_f = 1{,}134.90$~\euro\ per depot and week. Both lenses are evaluated against the same daily-delivery baseline and against the same solution, and they differ in how a vehicle-day below the depot peak is priced. The operator lens inherits the labour double count of Section~\ref{sec:cost_model} exactly as the routing lens does, which moves levels but not savings. Neither lens contains the service penalty $P$, which remains a steering term (Section~\ref{sec:schedule_opt}); Supplementary Section~S1 reports separately what happens if it is paid out.

\subsection{Schedule optimization and operator-cost polish}
\label{sec:schedule_opt}

For each \emph{cell} (each LSP, postal-code area pair), we enumerated all weekly delivery patterns feasible under a maximum holding time of $H_{\max}=3$ days over the six-day operating week, yielding $39$ distinct admissible patterns; the same candidate set is evaluated for every cell, while the resulting demand, waiting time, and cost differ by cell. For a pattern $\sigma$, a delivery day served its own demand plus the parcels held from the preceding non-delivery days, while non-delivery days carried no consolidated tour. The average customer waiting time $\bar{w}(\sigma)$ is the mean delay over the six weekdays between parcel arrival and the next scheduled service day under a cyclic interpretation of $\sigma$. Under the tour rule of Section~\ref{sec:tour_rule} a cell that reaches the parcel minimum is priced on its own, so most cells are cost-separable. A residual coupling remains inside the pooling groups: cells that fall below the minimum on a given day share that day's tour with their neighbours, so their cost depends on which of those neighbours also deliver that day. We therefore solved the selection by coordinate descent, run independently within each provider's hub network: at every update the focal cell chose the pattern that minimized the \emph{total} weekly surrogate cost of its hub, evaluated against the current schedules of all other cells at that hub, augmented by a linear service penalty,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:schedule_objective}
\sigma_z^{\star} = \arg\min_{\sigma \in \mathcal{S}} \left[ \underbrace{F_{h(z)}(\sigma \mid \sigma_{-z})}_{\text{hub cost, routing lens}} + \underbrace{P \, \theta_z \, p_z \, \bar{w}(\sigma)}_{\text{penalty on waiting}} \right],
\end{equation}

where $F_{h(z)}$ is the weekly routing-lens cost, Equation~\eqref{eq:lens_routing}, of the hub serving cell $z$, summed over the priced instances that the tour rule forms at that hub on each of the six weekdays. The sum is separable over cells wherever the tour rule leaves them standalone; it is not separable inside a pooling group, because the group's membership --- and hence the cost of every tour in it --- depends on which of its cells deliver on that day. Changing $\sigma_z$ therefore moves the focal cell's own tours and the tours of the groups it joins or leaves, while all other tours at the hub stay fixed. Retaining the hub objective rather than a per-cell one keeps this residual coupling priced. $\mathcal{S}$ is the set of feasible patterns, $\sigma_{-z}$ the fixed patterns of the other cells at the hub, $p_z$ the weekly parcel volume, and $\theta_z$ the share of parcels eligible for consolidation in the cell. Under a business-prioritized power law of the system-wide adoption $\theta$, business recipients opt in earlier than private ones, so each cell's $\theta_z$ reflects its own composition while the volume-weighted regional average equals $\theta$; only that fraction is consolidated. Coordinate descent iterates with multiple random restarts until no single-cell update improves the hub objective. The procedure is coordinate descent on a common hub objective, not a game between self-interested cells, so its fixed point is a coordinate-wise local optimum. Across five random restarts the objective agreed to within a relative $10^{-12}$, so the optimum is stable at the reported operating points, although global optimality is not guaranteed. As an audit of the residual coupling we re-derived, for every cell that exceeds the tour minimum on all six weekdays and is therefore priced standalone under any pattern, the pattern plain enumeration selects. Of $1{,}656$ cell--penalty pairs, $1{,}651$ agree exactly; the remaining five are accepted only because they satisfy a stated tolerance --- a single-day change of the pattern whose objective differs by at most $20$~\euro\ or $0.5\%$ of the cell's weekly objective, whichever is larger --- and all five arise where a paired move in the local search shifted the cell together with a pooled neighbour. They are near-ties, not a breach of the decoupling.
% src: results/revision_2026_08_v6/gates_report.md (G1a PASS: 1,656 checked, 0 hard,
%      5 tolerated); tolerances G1A_TOLERANCE_FLAT_EUR = 20.0, G1A_TOLERANCE_REL = 0.005
No parcels, routes, or vehicles are shared across providers, and system-wide figures are obtained solely by aggregating the independently optimized provider solutions. The service penalty $P$ is a shadow price \citep{BoydVandenberghe2004} of waiting --- the marginal cost the operator trades for one parcel-day of delay --- and enters \eqref{eq:schedule_objective} as a steering term only, which keeps the cost-service trade-off continuous. We swept $(P, \theta)$ over eight $P$ levels from $0$ to $10$~\euro/p/d and eleven $\theta$ levels from $0$ to $1$ in steps of $0.1$.


The coordinate-descent solution is the \emph{routing-optimal plan}, which we call stage~1. It minimizes per-day routing cost and is therefore free to concentrate deliveries on a few weekdays, which multiplies the depot peaks that the operator lens charges. A second stage polishes it in the operator's own currency. Stage~2 is a local search over (cell, pattern) moves that accepts a move if and only if
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:polish}
\Delta C^{\mathrm{op}} + P\, \theta_z\, p_z\, \Delta\bar{w} \;<\; 0 ,
\end{equation}
that is, if the change in variable cost, plus $6 c_f$ times the change in the touched depot's peak, plus the change in the service penalty, is strictly negative. There is no cost budget and no separate spread objective; every consequence of a move is priced. For $\theta > 0$ the search is \emph{frequency-free}: a cell may gain or lose delivery days, because Equation~\eqref{eq:polish} prices exactly what such a change touches --- distance and time, the depot peak, and waiting. At $\theta = 0$ nobody is willing to wait, the penalty vanishes for every $P$, and stage~2 is pinned to the daily plan so that the baseline is recovered exactly. The search is started three times, from the routing-optimal plan, from a range-balanced plan, and from a frequency-preserving polish, and the lowest operator cost is kept; this makes the result at least as good as the frequency-preserving variant by construction.

Two consequences must be stated plainly. First, stage~2 is a local search, not a global optimum, so we report the remaining gap to the flat-profile lower bound $\sum_h \lceil \text{vehicle-days}_h / 6 \rceil$, the peak a depot would need if its work were spread perfectly evenly over the week. Second, the operator plan is \emph{not} service-neutral with respect to the routing plan: because delivery days may be added or dropped, its mean delivery frequency and its average wait differ from the routing-optimal plan's, in either direction depending on the operating point. We therefore report both plans side by side under both lenses throughout Section~\ref{results}, and never combine a cost from one plan with a service figure from the other. The provider-level smoothing stage of the submitted version is dropped: vehicles do not move between depots, so the depot peaks already are the fleet, and redistributing weekdays across a provider's depots cannot lower them.

Under group $k$-fold cross-validation with folds grouped by postal-code area, so that no area appeared in both training and validation, the Daganzo-LightGBM hybrid (Daganzo-LGB-Hybrid) achieved 2.95\% MAPE ($R^2 = 0.997$). \subsection{Schedule optimization and the cost--service trade-off}
\label{sec:results_optim}

\autoref{fig:mix_init_vs_balanced} traces the optimized delivery-day distribution across the $312$ cells as $\theta$ rises; we summarize each scenario by the mean delivery frequency $\bar{f}$, the unweighted average number of weekly delivery days across cells. At $\theta = 0$ every cell is served daily at any penalty, recovering the baseline exactly: nobody is willing to wait, and stage~2 is pinned there by construction. As $\theta$ rises, $P$ sets the pace. In the routing-optimal plan at $(P, \theta) = (0, 1)$, $97.4\%$ of cells adopt the minimum two-day pattern ($\bar{f} = 2.03$); the remaining $2.6\%$ are high-volume urban cells whose two-day demand would breach vehicle capacity, so the Daganzo capacity term forces an extra day. The mode then shifts upward with $P$ ($\bar{f} = 4.12$ at $P = 0.5$, $\bar{f} = 5.01$ at $P = 1$); for $P \geq 5$ the routing-optimal plan is daily for all but $0.6\%$ of cells at full adoption, though not at low adoption, where $15.4\%$ remain non-daily at $(5, 0.1)$.

The operator-polished plan is a different schedule, not a re-timing of the same one, and the direction of the difference depends on the operating point. At low penalties it is markedly \emph{less} consolidated: at $(0, 1)$ the two-day share falls from $97.4$ to $72.4\%$ ($\bar{f} = 2.39$), because at depots that cannot rotate delivery days across cells more frequent delivery is the only way to lower a peak. At high penalties, where stage~1 delivers daily almost everywhere, the only available moves remove days, and the polish takes them wherever one removed peak vehicle ($1{,}134.90$~\euro\ per week) outweighs the penalty on the few parcels affected: at $(5, 1)$ it takes $4.8\%$ of cells off daily delivery ($\bar{f} = 5.95$) against $0.6\%$ in stage~1. Average waiting time consequently moves in both directions between the two plans --- down at $(0, 1)$ ($0.97$ to $0.77$~d), up at $(0.5, 1)$ ($0.20$ to $0.22$~d) --- and we make no directional claim about it; the wait of the operator plan is an outcome of cost minimization, not a service decision. Only at $\theta = 0$ is the question moot: the product $P \cdot \theta$ vanishes for every $P$, the polish is pinned, and the daily baseline is reproduced exactly.

Between these extremes the mix is not monotone in $\theta$, and the non-monotonicity is economic rather than numerical. At $P = 0.25$ the mean frequency \emph{rises} again from $\theta = 0.8$ to $0.9$, from $4.93$ to $5.16$ days in the routing-optimal plan and $4.68$ to $4.89$ in the operator plan, for two reasons: the penalty scales with $\theta$, so each dropped delivery day costs more as adoption grows; and the express residual thins out, from $9.6$ to $7.4\%$ of routing cost, until in small depots it no longer fills a vehicle and the residual tour of a skipped day costs nearly as much as the regular tour it replaces. At $\theta = 1$ the residual disappears altogether and the mean frequency drops to $3.10$ and $3.27$ days. The step from $\theta = 0.9$ to $1$ is therefore not a threshold in the willingness distribution but the price of the last non-willing customers, who must be served whatever the schedule and are disproportionately expensive to reach alone.

The submitted version reported a persistent saving in the high-penalty, low-adoption corner of the grid --- $3.6\%$ at $(P, \theta) = (10, 0.1)$ --- and explained it by the effective pressure $P \cdot \theta$ together with hub bundling. That explanation was wrong, and so was the number. The saving was an artefact of a structural asymmetry: only the consolidation scenarios could pool the standard-delivery parcels of cells without an own tour onto one shared depot tour, while the daily baseline paid a separate tour per cell and day, so at partial adoption roughly a quarter of all parcels rode a tour the baseline structurally could not have. With the universal tour rule of Section~\ref{sec:tour_rule} applied to both sides, the corner shrinks by almost an order of magnitude but does not disappear: at $(10, 0.1)$ the routing-optimal plan saves $0.40\%$ of routing cost and $0.36\%$ of operator cost, against $3.6\%$ reported, and $9.6\%$ of cells remain non-daily, against $42\%$. What survives at partial adoption is genuine but small, and it sits mainly in the operator lens.

All savings are taken against this grid's own daily baseline (\autoref{tab:two_lens}); the bundle head prices the pooled tours the baseline itself runs, which puts that denominator $0.6\%$ below the one obtained with the conservative fallback everywhere, so savings must never be normalized across grid versions. Three findings stand out. First, in the routing lens the picture of the submitted version survives: the routing-optimal plan saves $22.6\%$ at $(0, 1)$ and the saving decays monotonically with $P$. Second, in the operator lens that same plan is $8.4\%$ \emph{worse} than daily delivery. Its two-day patterns concentrate each depot's work on a few weekdays, so the summed depot peak rises by $34.5\%$; under weekly staffing the vehicle-days it avoids below the peak were never a cash saving. Third, the operator-cost polish reverses this: it saves $24.3\%$ of operator cost at $(0, 1)$ while cutting the summed depot peak by $16.9\%$, and it still realizes $20.0\%$ in the routing lens --- it gives up $2.7$~pp of routing saving to buy the peak reduction. The polish is never negative in the operator lens anywhere on the grid, whereas the routing-optimal plan turns negative there under partial adoption, down to $-10.3\%$ at $(0, 0.9)$. Partial adoption is thus worthwhile in the operator lens and close to neutral in the routing one.

Read as a pure cost objective, neither lens points to $P = 0.25$. For the operator-polished plan the saving falls monotonically with $P$ in both lenses; for the routing-optimal plan only the routing-lens saving does, its operator-lens column starting negative and peaking at $P = 0.5$. Read on its own plan, therefore, each lens prefers $P = 0$ on cost alone: the routing lens by $3.9$~pp and the operator lens by $1.7$~pp. We nevertheless recommend $P = 0.25$~\euro/p/d, because that is the point at which the service side is priced in. At $(0.25, 1)$ the operator plan saves $22.6\%$ of operator cost and $16.7\%$ of routing cost, cuts the summed depot peak by $17.2\%$ --- more deeply than the cost-optimal extreme does --- and halves the additional wait ($0.39$ against $0.77$~d). That halving costs $1.7$~pp of operator saving, and it stops being a trade at all once the delay is actually paid for: under a flat $0.50$~\euro\ discount per delayed parcel, $P = 0.25$ is the best point on the grid in the operator lens, ahead of $P = 0.5$ by $0.8$~pp, while the routing lens separates the two by $0.01$~pp, which is a tie at this grid's resolution (Supplementary Section~S1). On the fleet side, two related quantities must be kept apart: the summed depot peak, which is what the operator lens charges, and the system's own peak day, which \autoref{fig:grid_heatmap_6} (d) reports and which falls by $17.5\%$ at $(0, 1)$. The Monday--Saturday coefficient of variation of the system fleet falls from $0.139$ in the baseline to $0.023$ at $(0.25, 1)$ and $0.044$ at $(0.5, 1)$, that is by $84$ and $68\%$, with a grid maximum of $87\%$ at $(0, 0.7)$. Stage~2 is a local search and the gap it leaves is small but real: at $(0, 1)$ its summed depot peak of $1{,}030$ vehicles stands against a flat-profile lower bound of $980$, and at $(0.25, 1)$ the residual gap is $32$ peak vehicles. Read as a real payout rather than as a shadow price, the penalty halves the saving from $P = 0.25$ onwards but never removes it, and the break-even discount --- the most an operator could pay per delayed parcel and still break even --- rises with $P$ from $0.75$ to $2.25$~\euro\ in the operator lens and from $0.56$ to $1.21$~\euro\ in the routing lens, above the $50$ cents assumed here at every operating point (Supplementary Section~S1).

The mechanism behind the operator-lens result is depot structure rather than consolidation depth. Consolidation pays in the operator lens where a depot can \emph{rotate} delivery days across several cells, so their busy days fall on different weekdays and the peak stays flat; a depot serving a single cell has no rotation available, since under any two-day pattern its week is one large peak and one large trough, and its peak falls only when it delivers more often. Eight of DHL's sixteen depots serve exactly one cell, and the polish returns them to near-daily delivery, while DHL's largest depot, serving thirteen cells, keeps rotated two-day patterns. There, temporal consolidation saves kilometres but not fleet.

Three levels of evidence bear on whether the surrogate can be trusted at the operating points reported above, and they must not be merged.

At the instance level we re-routed $7{,}610$ instances with the solver: the complete daily baseline, and at full adoption the operator-polished plan at $P \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75\}$ together with the routing-optimal plan at $P \in \{0, 0.25\}$. One instance returned a partial solution --- a DHL depot with a single unassigned job --- and every figure below is reported on the \emph{clean} basis, which drops that instance from the error statistics and from the cost totals alike; keeping it lowers the one saving it touches from $20.6$ to $19.9\%$ and changes nothing else. Over the $7{,}609$ clean instances the surrogate's error is $3.51\%$ MAPE at a bias of $+3.05\%$, and the bias is positive in every group we report: the model over-prices. It over-prices most where tours are thinnest, that is in the daily baseline ($5.80\%$ MAPE at $+5.39\%$ mean per-tour bias; $+4.88\%$ on single-cell tours and $+14.50\%$ on pooled ones), and least at the consolidated points ($2.85\%$ MAPE at $+2.39\%$, between $2.3$ and $3.7\%$ MAPE per point). The second level is the bundle head's out-of-fold certification of Section~\ref{sec:tour_rule}, which bounds the pricing error where the head is deployed but is a cross-validation result rather than a re-routing.

The third level is what those errors do to the savings, and here the revision corrects the submitted version rather than confirming it. Because the baseline is over-priced by $4.4\%$ in the routing lens and $4.0\%$ in the operator lens while the consolidated points are over-priced by only $1.2$ to $2.9\%$ and $0.5$ to $2.5\%$ respectively --- these are total-cost gaps, not the mean per-tour biases above --- the denominator falls faster than the numerator, and every predicted saving is an \emph{upper bound}. At full adoption the operator-polished plan realizes $17.5$, $14.5$, $10.6$ and $7.7\%$ of routing saving at $P = 0$, $0.25$, $0.5$ and $0.75$ against $20.0$, $16.7$, $12.3$ and $9.0\%$ predicted, and $22.1$, $20.7$, $16.3$ and $12.8\%$ of operator saving against $24.3$, $22.6$, $17.8$ and $14.1\%$; the routing-optimal plan realizes $20.6$ and $16.4\%$ of routing saving against $22.6$ and $18.7\%$. The shortfall is $1.3$ to $2.5$~pp for the operator-polished plan and $2.1$ to $3.7$~pp for the routing-optimal one, and it changes no sign and no ordering. The submitted version reported the opposite direction --- a realized $24.3\%$ against $22.8\%$ predicted at $P = 0$, and called the surrogate conservative --- on the pipeline that predates the universal tour rule; this is the first validation to re-route the daily baseline itself, and the baseline's own over-pricing is what reverses the sign of the correction.

Two consequences are worth stating. The central two-lens result is not weakened by the correction but sharpened: the operator-lens penalty of the routing-optimal plan is larger in reality than predicted, $-12.1\%$ against $-8.4\%$ at $(0, 1)$ and $+5.2\%$ against $+7.8\%$ at $(0.25, 1)$, so a plan chosen on per-day routing cost is a worse weekly proposition than the surrogate suggests. And the vehicle count $\lceil p/Q \rceil$ that both lenses rest on is reproduced almost exactly: predicted against actual summed depot peaks are $1{,}239/1{,}249$ at the baseline and $1{,}030/1{,}032$ at $(0, 1)$, and they never differ by more than five vehicles in a thousand at the six consolidated settings, or by ten in the baseline's $1{,}239$. The fleet-side conclusions therefore carry the accuracy of a counting rule, not of a cost model.

Partial adoption was validated separately, at $(P, \theta) = (0.25, 0.5)$ on the operator-polished plan, over a stratified subset of $1{,}000$ of that point's $1{,}594$ instances --- every instance of the three smallest providers plus at least half of each remaining provider's demand, drawn round-robin over instance-kind and size strata. There the error is strongly kind-dependent: $4.33\%$ MAPE at $+3.96\%$ bias on single-cell delivery tours and $7.43\%$ at $+6.52\%$ on pooled ones, but $12.90\%$ at $+12.03\%$ on single-cell \emph{express} tours and $22.64\%$ at $+21.46\%$ on pooled express tours. The express residual is the part of a partial-adoption plan that only consolidation creates, so this bias works in the opposite direction to the one at full adoption: it inflates the cost of the consolidated schedule and makes consolidation look \emph{less} attractive than it is. The two roughly offset --- the point is over-priced by $5.3\%$ in total against the baseline's $4.4\%$, and its summed depot peak is reproduced to within $1\%$ ($811$ predicted against $819$) --- but we do not quote a realized saving here, because a $1{,}000$-instance subset cannot be divided by the full baseline.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Six limitations and one methodological caveat bound these results. (i) The operator-lens outcome is shaped by depot structure, and one carrier dominates it: eight of DHL's sixteen depots serve a single cell, and DHL operates the only multi-depot network in the case study. The rotation mechanism is therefore evidenced within a single carrier's network, and neither its magnitude nor its generality can be established from these data. (ii) Pooling is strictly within a depot, since every tour starts and ends at the depot of its cells, so the model cannot exploit cooperation between neighbouring depots or between providers; lifting that restriction is the most promising direction for future work and would raise the achievable savings. (iii) Stage~2 is a local search, and we report its gap to the flat-profile bound rather than claiming optimality. (iv) All optimization is surrogate-based. The full-adoption plans are solver-validated and their savings are reported both as predicted and as realized; the partial-adoption regime is validated only at one point and only on a subset of its instances, so its savings remain predictions, and the express tours that regime creates are the least accurately priced part of the model (Section~\ref{sec:results_validation}). (v) The bundle head prices $53.9\%$ of the pooled tours that occur and $27.2\%$ of the pooled cost; elsewhere a pooled tour is charged as the sum of its members' single-cell prices, which is conservative but coarse, and the certification is specific to this region's cell geometry. (vi) Fleet accounting changed with the tour rule: the baseline Monday--Saturday coefficient of variation is $0.139$ here against $0.135$ in the submitted version, so fleet-smoothness figures are comparable within a grid version but not across versions. All levels, in both lenses, additionally inherit the labour double count of the label cost model, which leaves savings unaffected; Supplementary Section~S2 states its size. The methodological caveat concerns feature construction: for six of the seven providers the per-stop demand array falls back to one parcel per demand point, so two of the $25$ features are populated differently at run time than during training. The deployed model was validated end-to-end in exactly this state ($3.51\%$ MAPE at $+3.05\%$ bias over $7{,}609$ solver-routed instances, Section~\ref{sec:results_validation}), so the reported numbers stand, but the skew costs accuracy that a re-trained model would recover.







\section{Conclusion}
\label{concl}

We presented an ML-based surrogate optimization framework for time-based consolidation in last-mile parcel delivery, on a case study of $312$ provider--postal-code cells formed by seven LSPs over $48$ postal-code areas and $1.26$~million weekly parcels in the Hanover Region. The Daganzo-LightGBM hybrid achieved $2.95\%$ MAPE under postal-code-grouped cross-validation, and a universal minimum/maximum tour rule keeps every priced tour inside the instance family it was trained on, removing the pooling asymmetry that inflated the submitted version's partial-adoption results.

The central result of this revision is that the answer depends on the cost lens. Under a routing lens that charges every vehicle-day in full, the cost-optimal plan saves $22.6\%$ at full adoption. Under an operator lens, in which drivers are employed for the week and only the depot peak carries the fixed bill, the very same plan is $8.4\%$ worse than daily delivery, because its two-day patterns raise the summed depot peak by $34.5\%$. Re-optimizing the second stage in the operator's own currency --- a frequency-free local search that accepts a move only if variable cost, peak cost, and waiting penalty jointly fall --- turns this into $24.3\%$ of operator saving at a $16.9\%$ lower summed depot peak, for $2.7$~pp of forgone routing saving. Consolidation pays in the operator lens where a depot can rotate delivery days across several cells; where a depot serves a single cell it returns to near-daily delivery, because there consolidation saves kilometres but no fleet.

On cost alone each lens, read on its own plan, prefers the cost-optimal extreme, by $3.9$~pp in the routing lens and $1.7$~pp in the operator lens. We nevertheless recommend $P = 0.25$~\euro/p/d, which retains $22.6\%$ of operator saving at half the additional wait and a slightly deeper peak reduction, and which a flat $0.50$~\euro\ discount per delayed parcel turns into the best point on the grid outright in the operator lens, by $0.8$~pp, with the routing lens rating it level with $P = 0.5$. Per-LSP chord-distance optima still sort the operators into service-bound, hybrid, and cost-aggressive classes in the routing lens, but the sorting is itself lens-dependent: under the operator lens two carriers move up one class and a third leaves the class band entirely. Where consolidation is worth having is set by geography and by depot structure together: it pays most in sparse cells far from the depot, and it pays in the operator lens where a depot can rotate delivery days across several cells, while in the one multi-depot network of the case study a depot serving a single cell saves kilometres but no fleet. For practice this suggests differentiating the service by provider and depot rather than offering it uniformly, and --- decisively --- planning the weekly roster together with the schedule, because a plan that is cost-optimal per day can be loss-making per week. All figures above are the surrogate's; re-routing them puts the realized savings $1.3$ to $3.7$~pp lower (Section~\ref{sec:results_validation}). Validation relies on VROOM as a routing benchmark rather than on ground truth, and the partial-adoption regime with pooled tours is not yet solver-validated. Pooling also remains within a depot, so inter-depot and inter-provider cooperation, which could amplify the gains, is left to future work.
