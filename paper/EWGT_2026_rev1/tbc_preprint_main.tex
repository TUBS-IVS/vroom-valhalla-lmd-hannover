\begin{abstract}
Time-based consolidation (TBC) lets logistics service providers delay suitable parcels for batched delivery, trading customer waiting time against operational savings. While the principle is well known, its cost-service balance, its spatial signature, and its fleet-side benefits across heterogeneous carriers remain insufficiently quantified. We optimize TBC schedules on the Hanover Region case ($312$ provider--postal-code cells formed by seven LSPs over $48$ postal-code areas, $1.26$~million weekly parcels) using a machine-learning surrogate that couples Daganzo's continuum approximation with a LightGBM residual ($2.95\%$ mean absolute percentage error, MAPE, under postal-code-grouped cross-validation), embedded in a coordinate-descent search over a service-penalty $P$ and a willingness-to-wait share $\theta$. Every priced tour --- in the daily baseline exactly as in every consolidation scenario --- obeys one universal minimum/maximum rule, so no scenario may pool sparse deliveries in a way the baseline cannot. We report two cost lenses: a \emph{routing} lens that charges the full daily vehicle cost, and an \emph{operator} lens that charges only variable cost below each depot's weekly fleet peak. Under the routing lens, savings reach \provisional{$23.1\%$} at the cost-optimal extreme; under the operator lens the same routing-optimal plan is \provisional{$7.8\%$} \emph{worse} than daily delivery, because two-day patterns multiply depot peaks. A second-stage operator-cost polish turns this into \provisional{$24.7\%$} operator saving at a \provisional{$16.9\%$} lower peak fleet. In both lenses the saving falls with the service penalty at full adoption, so on cost alone each prefers $P = 0$, by \provisional{$3.9$}~pp in the routing lens and \provisional{$1.9$}~pp in the operator lens. Once waiting is priced we nonetheless recommend $P = 0.25$~\euro\ per parcel-day: it halves the additional wait for \provisional{$1.9$}~pp of operator saving, and it becomes the best point on the grid as soon as a flat $0.50$~\euro\ discount per delayed parcel is actually paid out. Three carrier classes emerge with distinct sweet spots in $P \in [0.25, 0.75]$~\euro\ per parcel-day, and their ordering itself depends on the lens; under the operator-polished plan the Monday--Saturday fleet coefficient of variation drops by \provisional{$52$--$83\%$} across that range. Consolidation pays in the operator lens where a depot can rotate delivery days across several of its cells, whereas depots serving a single cell revert to near-daily delivery and save kilometres but no fleet. Out-of-sample VROOM re-routing shows the surrogate is conservative --- its mean bias is $+1.8\%$ on daily and $+2.5$ to $+3.5\%$ on consolidated schedules across $1{,}247$ solver-routed cells --- so the reported savings are understated rather than overstated.







\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Related Work}
\label{intro}

In the European Union, $78\%$ of internet users purchased goods or services online in 2025 \citep{Eurostat2025}, illustrating how deeply parcel delivery is embedded in everyday consumption. Each online order creates a physical delivery task in the final segment of the parcel supply chain, commonly referred to as last-mile delivery (LMD), which is among the most expensive, inefficient, and environmentally burdensome parts of the supply chain \citep{Olsson2019}. For logistics service providers (LSPs), this intensifies existing pressures: LMD operations are already characterized by low productivity, restrictive delivery time windows, complex urban operating conditions, and high cost shares \citep{BACHOFNER2022100020}, reinforced by climate change, rapid urbanization, demographic change, and shifting demand patterns \citep{logistics2040025}.

At the same time, e-commerce has made delivery speed a visible part of the customer-facing service promise, so LSPs are expected to provide faster, more personalized, and more reliable services while prices and margins remain constrained \citep{Vivaldini}. However, not all customers prioritize the fastest possible delivery: \citet{Nguyen19} distinguish price-oriented, convenience-oriented, and value-for-money-oriented segments, indicating that some customers may accept slower delivery if it lowers cost. This heterogeneity makes delivery timing a potential operational control variable. Temporarily storing eligible parcels and dispatching them jointly can reduce costs compared with direct delivery \citep{PENG2023109044}, and a some-day delivery option can reduce cost and vehicle requirements when customers accept a latest delivery day \citep{Voigt2025SomeDayDelivery}; sustainability benefits, however, arise only if the temporal flexibility translates into better routing, lower fleet requirements, or reduced travel distances \citep{Zeelenberg2026WillingnessToWait}. Time-based consolidation (TBC) packages this trade-off into a delivery concept in which eligible customers accept a longer delivery window, for example in exchange for a lower price \citep{Naeimian2026TemporalConsolidation}, so that eligible shipments are temporarily held and grouped into batched deliveries within a predefined service promise. TBC therefore moves away from a uniform immediacy logic toward a differentiated service design in which customers retain choice while LSPs gain temporal flexibility.

This paper investigates TBC as an operational response to the tension between customer expectations, LSP constraints, and sustainability objectives. From a planning perspective, the core task is to assign eligible parcels to delivery days at the postal-code-area level such that delivery density increases, additional waiting time remains limited, and weekday demand peaks or uneven fleet use are avoided. Because each schedule induces a different demand pattern and thus different delivery routes, direct evaluation would require solving many Vehicle Routing Problems, which is computationally infeasible given the VRP's NP-hardness \citep{LenstraRinnooyKan1981}. We therefore propose an ML-based surrogate-optimization framework for identifying cost-efficient TBC schedules in a large-scale case study of the Hanover Region in Germany. The framework combines a postal-code-area scheduling model with a routing-cost surrogate trained on VRP solutions; building on ML-supported combinatorial optimization \citep{BENGIO2021405} and data-driven extensions of analytical LMD route-distance approximations \citep{Figliozzi08, KIRUBAKARAN2026101720}, it enables efficient schedule search across adoption scenarios. We make four contributions: a hybrid Daganzo--LightGBM routing-cost surrogate that couples an interpretable continuum backbone with a learned residual and is applied only inside a measured validity domain; a universal minimum/maximum tour rule that makes the daily baseline and every consolidation scenario structurally comparable; a scalable optimization of spatially and provider-differentiated TBC delivery-day patterns under two explicit cost lenses, per-day routing cost and weekly operator cost with staffed depot peaks; and empirical evidence on where TBC pays off, and on how the choice of lens changes both the recommended operating point and the ordering of carriers. Routing and demand simulation enter only as label generation and out-of-sample evaluation, so the framework is a surrogate-assisted delivery-schedule optimization rather than a simulation study.

\section{Methodology}
\label{methods}

Our framework used weekly parcel-demand patterns generated with our own HAGRID demand model \citep{BIENZEISLER2026104682}, covering seven LSPs in the Hanover Region (about $1.26$~million parcels and $1.91$~M\euro\ of baseline operating cost per week). As \autoref{fig:input_data} shows, demand varies strongly between dense urban and sparse rural postal-code areas, peaks midweek, and is lowest on Saturday. To make repeated routing evaluations tractable, we decomposed the study area into postal-code-level routing subproblems while preserving the demand, spatial, and depot-access characteristics relevant for delivery cost. The pipeline structure is summarized in \autoref{fig:framework}.\footnote{The implementation of the proposed framework is available at: \url{https://github.com/TUBS-IVS/vroom-valhalla-lmd-hannover}}


The unit of analysis is a \emph{cell}, defined as an LSP--postal-code-area pair assigned to the provider's serving hub and described by daily demand, area size, stop structure, and depot-access distance. The case study comprises $312$ such cells over $48$ postal-code areas, each area served by up to seven LSPs; all distributional statements below are per cell unless stated otherwise. The decision variable is the weekly \emph{delivery pattern}, i.e., the service weekdays for that cell; parcels from non-service days are held until the next scheduled service day. LSP operating costs are route-dependent. Following open-data-based LMD modeling \citep{HORL2025104893}, we generated routing-cost labels with VROOM \citep{vroom_v1.14} based on Valhalla-derived travel times and distances on OpenStreetMap road geometry.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/valhalla/valhalla}} Recurrent congestion was approximated by applying an empirically derived correction factor to free-flow travel times. The current daily-delivery operation served as the baseline for all consolidation scenarios.

\subsection{Cost approximation and machine-learning surrogate}
\label{sec:cost_model}

Solving a full VRP for every candidate schedule is computationally too costly, so we approximated operational cost with a continuous model based on Daganzo's distance formulation \citep{Daganzo84} and corrected it with a data-driven term. For area $z$ on day $d$, route distance and cost are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:daganzo}
\hat{D}_{z,d} = m_{z,d}\!\left( 2 r_z + \beta\sqrt{\tfrac{n_{z,d}}{m_{z,d}}\, A_z} \right), \qquad
\hat{C}_{z,d} = c_f\, m_{z,d} + c_d\, \hat{D}_{z,d},
\end{equation}
with $m_{z,d}=\lceil p_{z,d}/Q\rceil$ tours (parcel volume $p_{z,d}$, vehicle capacity $Q = 230$), average depot distance $r_z$, $n_{z,d}$ stops over area $A_z$, and network/shape factor $\beta$: each of the $m_{z,d}$ tours travels to the area and back ($2r_z$) and serves its $n_{z,d}/m_{z,d}$ stops across the service area. The rate $c_d$ prices distance and $c_f$ is the fixed daily cost per vehicle. Consolidation lowers cost by raising delivery density while the capacity term penalizes over-consolidation.

Such approximations reproduce the first-order scaling but are point-inaccurate, as they abstract from the road network, depot position, and demand clustering, normally requiring manual recalibration \citep{Figliozzi08}. Instead, we used Equation~\eqref{eq:daganzo} as a physically interpretable \emph{backbone} and learned its deviation from solver cost,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:surrogate}
\tilde{C}_{z,d} = \alpha\,\hat{C}_{z,d} + g(\mathbf{x}_{z,d}),
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is a global calibration factor and the structured residual $g(\cdot)$ is a LightGBM ensemble \citep{NIPS20176449f44a}, chosen because gradient-boosted trees handle tabular nonlinearities and feature interactions well, and expose feature importance for interpretation \citep{10.5555/3600270.3600307}. The scalar $\alpha$ was calibrated once as the median ratio of solver cost to the raw Daganzo prediction across the training pool, and $g(\cdot)$ then learned the structured remainder.

The quantity $\tilde{C}$ predicts is fixed by what the solver labels contain, and it is worth stating exactly. Every label --- and therefore every predicted, baseline, and validated cost reported in this paper --- follows the effective cost model $189.15$~\euro\ per vehicle and day, $0.3864$~\euro\ per kilometre, and $36$~\euro\ per route-hour, the last because VROOM's default per-hour rate was active in all labels; the three terms contribute $72.2$, $6.0$, and $21.7\%$ of label cost. The Daganzo backbone in Equation~\eqref{eq:daganzo} carries only the vehicle and distance terms, so $\alpha$ and the learned residual absorb the route-time component. The driver's wage is part of $c_f$, but the route-time charge is separate and additional, and is not contained in it. The consequence is that driver labour is charged twice: once inside $c_f$ and once through the route-time term. Baseline, scenarios, prediction, and out-of-sample validation all carry that term, so the comparison is internally consistent and the \emph{relative} savings we report are unaffected --- route time is dominated by per-parcel service time, which barely moves between daily and batched operation, so a near-constant term sits in numerator and denominator alike and damps rather than inflates the percentages. Absolute cost \emph{levels}, by contrast, are high by roughly the time share, about a fifth. Re-labelling the training pool with the per-hour rate set to zero, and re-training on it, is left to future work; it would lower the levels without materially changing the comparisons made here.

The feature vector $\mathbf{x}_{z,d}$ comprised 25 demand and geometry descriptors derived ex ante from the data, following the taxonomy of \citet{Akkerman2025} (parcel volume, stops, service-area size, depot distance, parcels per stop, the load factor $p_{z,d}/Q$, and per-stop demand statistics), with VROOM/Valhalla route costs as training targets. To cover the consolidation cost range rather than only baseline operations, we augmented the pool with synthetic cell-day samples obtained by demand scaling, stop dropout, coordinate jitter, and multi-day aggregation, the last reproducing the elevated per-day volumes of batched delivery. Generalization to both sparse rural and dense urban areas was assessed by group $k$-fold cross-validation with folds grouped by postal-code area.

\subsection{Tour geometry and the validity domain of the surrogate}
\label{sec:tour_rule}

A surrogate may only be asked to price instances of the family it was trained on. Our training pool aggregates demand in \emph{time} --- up to three days of one postal-code area --- but never in \emph{space}: it contains no multi-area instance. We therefore constrain every tour the model prices, in the daily baseline exactly as in every consolidation scenario, by one universal rule. A tour carries at least $230$ parcels, one vehicle load, below which the out-of-fold error of the surrogate triples to $7.1\%$; at most $556$ stops, the $99$th percentile of the training pool; at most $159$~km$^2$ of service area, the $95$th percentile, beyond which the surrogate's bias drifts negative, reaching $-4.3\%$ at the training maximum of $358$~km$^2$; and its members' convex hull may not exceed $1.22$ times their summed area, the largest ratio observed in the training pool. A cell that reaches the parcel minimum on its own is always served alone. Cells below it are pooled with neighbouring cells of the same provider served by the same depot until the rule is met, greedily by proximity and subject to the three caps; the pooling is recomputed for every weekday and applies to the baseline as well.

Two properties matter for the interpretation of the results. First, the rule is \emph{scenario-blind} by construction: there is no code path that lets a consolidation scenario pool the deliveries of sparse areas while the daily baseline pays a separate tour per area and day. That asymmetry was present in the submitted version and inflated the savings reported at partial adoption; it is removed here. Second, non-consolidated parcels are not handled by a separate mechanism. On a day on which a cell runs no batched tour, the parcels of its recipients who did not opt in form an ordinary instance of that cell and enter the same partition, so they are pooled or served alone under the same rule as any other tour.

Pooled instances lie outside the single-area training family, so they are priced by a dedicated \emph{bundle head}: the same $\alpha$-calibrated Daganzo backbone with a LightGBM residual, trained on $877$ solver-routed pooled tours and validated out-of-fold with folds grouped by member set. The head prices a pooled tour only in bins in which it is certified --- at least $6$ labels and an absolute out-of-fold bias of at most $5\%$ --- which is $21$ of $235$ bins, covering $44.0\%$ of the pooled instances that actually occur in the optimized solutions, with $2.85\%$ out-of-fold MAPE ($2.71\%$ occurrence-weighted) and $1.35\%$ absolute bias; pool-wide, that is including the uncertified bins, the head reaches $5.37\%$ MAPE at $+0.57\%$ bias. Outside its certified support the head refuses to price and the pooled tour is charged as the sum of its members' single-cell prices, a deliberately conservative fallback that is counted and reported per operating point.






\subsection{Two cost lenses}
\label{sec:lenses}

A weekly schedule is not evaluated the same way by a route planner and by a depot manager, so we report every result under two lenses. Let $i$ index the priced instances of a solution --- single-cell tours and pooled tours alike --- with surrogate cost $\tilde{C}_i$ and vehicle requirement $v_i = \lceil p_i / Q \rceil$, and let $v_{h,d}$ be the vehicles a depot $h$ needs on weekday $d$. The \emph{routing lens} is the accounting of the submitted version: every vehicle-day is charged in full,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lens_routing}
C^{\mathrm{route}} = \sum_i \tilde{C}_i .
\end{equation}
The \emph{operator lens} instead reflects weekly staffing. Drivers are employed and vans are owned for the week, so a depot pays its fixed bill for the peak day and only the variable part of every other vehicle-day,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lens_operator}
C^{\mathrm{op}} = \underbrace{\sum_i \bigl( \tilde{C}_i - c_f\, v_i \bigr)}_{\text{variable (distance and time)}} \; + \; \underbrace{6\, c_f \sum_h \max_d v_{h,d}}_{\text{weekly fixed cost of the peak fleet}},
\end{equation}
with $c_f = 189.15$~\euro, so a peak vehicle costs $6 c_f = 1{,}134.90$~\euro\ per depot and week. Both lenses are evaluated against the same daily-delivery baseline and against the same solution, and they differ in how a vehicle-day below the depot peak is priced. One caveat follows from the label cost model: the route-time charge stays inside the variable bucket while $6 c_f$ already contains the weekly wage, so the operator lens counts labour twice and its absolute levels are inflated by roughly the same fifth as in the routing lens. Savings in either lens are unaffected, because the inflated term appears in the baseline and in every scenario alike. Neither lens contains the service penalty $P$, which remains a steering term (Section~\ref{sec:schedule_opt}); Section~\ref{sec:results_discount} reports separately what happens if it is paid out.

\subsection{Schedule optimization and operator-cost polish}
\label{sec:schedule_opt}

For each \emph{cell} (each LSP, postal-code area pair), we enumerated all weekly delivery patterns feasible under a maximum holding time of $H_{\max}=3$ days over the six-day operating week, yielding $39$ distinct admissible patterns; the same candidate set is evaluated for every cell, while the resulting demand, waiting time, and cost differ by cell. For a pattern $\sigma$, a delivery day served its own demand plus the parcels held from the preceding non-delivery days, while non-delivery days carried no consolidated tour. The average customer waiting time $\bar{w}(\sigma)$ is the mean delay over the six weekdays between parcel arrival and the next scheduled service day under a cyclic interpretation of $\sigma$. Under the tour rule of Section~\ref{sec:tour_rule} a cell that reaches the parcel minimum is priced on its own, so most cells are cost-separable. A residual coupling remains inside the pooling groups: cells that fall below the minimum on a given day share that day's tour with their neighbours, so their cost depends on which of those neighbours also deliver that day. We therefore solved the selection by coordinate descent, run independently within each provider's hub network: at every update the focal cell chose the pattern that minimized the \emph{total} weekly surrogate cost of its hub, evaluated against the current schedules of all other cells at that hub, augmented by a linear service penalty,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:schedule_objective}
\sigma_z^{\star} = \arg\min_{\sigma \in \mathcal{S}} \left[ \underbrace{F_{h(z)}(\sigma \mid \sigma_{-z})}_{\text{hub cost, routing lens}} + \underbrace{P \, \theta_z \, p_z \, \bar{w}(\sigma)}_{\text{penalty on waiting}} \right],
\end{equation}

where $F_{h(z)}$ is the weekly routing-lens cost, Equation~\eqref{eq:lens_routing}, of the hub serving cell $z$, summed over the priced instances that the tour rule forms at that hub on each of the six weekdays. The sum is separable over cells wherever the tour rule leaves them standalone; it is not separable inside a pooling group, because the group's membership --- and hence the cost of every tour in it --- depends on which of its cells deliver on that day. Changing $\sigma_z$ therefore moves the focal cell's own tours and the tours of the groups it joins or leaves, while all other tours at the hub stay fixed. Retaining the hub objective rather than a per-cell one keeps this residual coupling priced. $\mathcal{S}$ is the set of feasible patterns, $\sigma_{-z}$ the fixed patterns of the other cells at the hub, $p_z$ the weekly parcel volume, and $\theta_z$ the share of parcels eligible for consolidation in the cell. Following a business-prioritized power law of the system-wide adoption $\theta$, business recipients opt in earlier than private ones, so each cell's $\theta_z$ reflects its own composition while the volume-weighted regional average equals $\theta$; only this willing fraction is consolidated, the rest delivered conventionally. Coordinate descent iterates with multiple random restarts until no single-cell update improves the hub objective. The procedure is therefore coordinate descent on a common hub objective, not a game between self-interested cells; its fixed point is a coordinate-wise local optimum---equivalently, a Nash equilibrium of an identical-interest game. Across five random restarts the objective agreed to within a relative $10^{-12}$, so the optimum is stable at the reported operating points, although global optimality is not guaranteed. The optimization runs independently within each LSP's own hub network; no parcels, routes, or vehicles are shared across providers, and system-wide figures are obtained solely by aggregating the independently optimized provider solutions. The service penalty $P$ is a shadow price \citep{BoydVandenberghe2004} of waiting, i.e.\ the marginal cost the operator trades for one additional parcel-day of delay, and enters \eqref{eq:schedule_objective} as a steering term only, not as a cost component, which keeps the cost-service trade-off continuous across the grid. We swept $(P, \theta)$ over eight $P$ levels from $0$ to $10$~\euro/p/d and eleven $\theta$ levels from $0$ to $1$ in steps of $0.1$.


The coordinate-descent solution is the \emph{routing-optimal plan}, which we call stage~1. It minimizes per-day routing cost and is therefore free to concentrate deliveries on a few weekdays, which multiplies the depot peaks that the operator lens charges. A second stage polishes it in the operator's own currency. Stage~2 is a local search over (cell, pattern) moves that accepts a move if and only if
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:polish}
\Delta C^{\mathrm{op}} + P\, \theta_z\, p_z\, \Delta\bar{w} \;<\; 0 ,
\end{equation}
that is, if the change in variable cost, plus $6 c_f$ times the change in the touched depot's peak, plus the change in the service penalty, is strictly negative. There is no cost budget and no separate spread objective; every consequence of a move is priced. For $\theta > 0$ the search is \emph{frequency-free}: a cell may gain or lose delivery days, because Equation~\eqref{eq:polish} prices exactly what such a change touches --- distance and time, the depot peak, and waiting. At $\theta = 0$ nobody is willing to wait, the penalty vanishes for every $P$, and stage~2 is pinned to the daily plan so that the baseline is recovered exactly. The search is started three times, from the routing-optimal plan, from a range-balanced plan, and from a frequency-preserving polish, and the lowest operator cost is kept; this makes the result at least as good as the frequency-preserving variant by construction.

Two consequences must be stated plainly. First, stage~2 is a local search, not a global optimum, so we report the remaining gap to the flat-profile lower bound $\sum_h \lceil \text{vehicle-days}_h / 6 \rceil$, the peak a depot would need if its work were spread perfectly evenly over the week. Second, the operator plan is \emph{not} service-neutral with respect to the routing plan: because delivery days may be added or dropped, its mean delivery frequency and its average customer waiting time differ from those of the routing-optimal plan, in either direction depending on the operating point. We therefore report both plans side by side under both lenses throughout Section~\ref{results}, and never combine a cost taken from one plan with a service figure taken from the other. The provider-level smoothing stage of the submitted version is dropped from the production path: vehicles do not move between depots, so the depot peaks already are the fleet, and a stage that only redistributes weekdays across a provider's depots cannot lower them.

\subsection{Surrogate model: quality and validation}
\label{sec:results_surrogate}



Under group $k$-fold cross-validation with folds grouped by postal-code area, so that no area appeared in both training and validation, the Daganzo-LightGBM hybrid (Daganzo-LGB-Hybrid) achieved 2.95\% MAPE ($R^2 = 0.997$). We kept the Daganzo cost parameters at standard operational values and calibrated the global cost level through the factor $\alpha$, defined as the median ratio of solver cost to Daganzo cost ($\alpha = 1.34$). A single global $\alpha$ suffices: replacing it with per-LSP factors did not improve out-of-fold accuracy ($3.2\%$ vs.\ $2.9\%$ MAPE), because the LightGBM residual already absorbs provider-specific scale. \autoref{fig:surrogate_progression} reports the out-of-fold accuracy in three stages: the uncalibrated approximation underestimated cost by about 26\% (panel a); the global $\alpha$ calibration removed this systematic bias and reached 9.7\% MAPE (panel b); and the learned LightGBM residual captured remaining effects related to capacity thresholds, stop density, and depot distance, reducing the error to 2.95\% with near-zero bias (panel c). Overall, among the benchmark models tested in this study, including both statistical and ML approaches, the Daganzo-LGB-Hybrid achieved the best predictive performance. The banded structure in \autoref{fig:surrogate_progression} reflects the integer tour count $\lceil p/Q \rceil$, which quantizes the Daganzo backbone into cost bands, together with families of augmented samples sharing a postal-code area; it is a property of the training pool rather than a fit artifact, and the postal-code-grouped folds keep all samples of an area on the same side of every split.

\subsection{Schedule optimization and the cost--service trade-off}
\label{sec:results_optim}

\autoref{fig:mix_init_vs_balanced} traces the optimized delivery-day distribution across the $312$ cells as $\theta$ rises; we summarize each scenario by the mean delivery frequency $\bar{f}$, the unweighted average number of weekly delivery days across cells. At $\theta = 0$ every cell is served daily at any penalty, recovering the baseline exactly: nobody is willing to wait, and stage~2 is pinned there by construction. As $\theta$ rises, $P$ sets the pace. In the routing-optimal plan at $(P, \theta) = (0, 1)$, \provisional{$97.4\%$} of cells adopt the minimum two-day pattern (\provisional{$\bar{f} = 2.03$}); the remaining \provisional{$2.6\%$} are high-volume urban cells whose two-day demand would breach vehicle capacity, so the Daganzo capacity term forces an extra day. The mode then shifts upward with $P$ (\provisional{$\bar{f} = 3.95$} at $P = 0.5$, \provisional{$\bar{f} = 4.93$} at $P = 1$); for $P \geq 5$ and $\theta \geq 0.3$ the routing-optimal plan reverts to fully daily delivery.

The operator-polished plan is a different schedule, not a re-timing of the same one, and the direction of the difference depends on the operating point. At low penalties it is markedly \emph{less} consolidated: at $(0, 1)$ the two-day share falls from \provisional{$97.4$} to \provisional{$72.8\%$} (\provisional{$\bar{f} = 2.38$}), because at depots that cannot rotate delivery days across cells more frequent delivery is the only way to lower a peak. At high penalties, where stage~1 already delivers daily everywhere, the only available moves remove days, and the polish takes them wherever one removed peak vehicle ($1{,}134.90$~\euro\ per week) outweighs the penalty on the few parcels affected: at $(5, 1)$ it takes \provisional{$4.8\%$} of cells off daily delivery (\provisional{$\bar{f} = 5.95$}). Average waiting time consequently moves in both directions between the two plans --- down at $(0, 1)$ (\provisional{$0.97$} to \provisional{$0.77$}~d), up at $(0.5, 1)$ (\provisional{$0.21$} to \provisional{$0.23$}~d) --- and we make no directional claim about it; the wait of the operator plan is an outcome of cost minimization, not a service decision. Only at $\theta = 0$ is the question moot: the product $P \cdot \theta$ vanishes for every $P$, the polish is pinned, and the daily baseline is reproduced exactly.

The submitted version reported a persistent saving in the high-penalty, low-adoption corner of the grid --- $3.6\%$ at $(P, \theta) = (10, 0.1)$ --- and explained it by the effective pressure $P \cdot \theta$ together with hub bundling. That explanation was wrong, and so was the number. The saving was an artefact of a structural asymmetry: only the consolidation scenarios were allowed to pool the standard-delivery parcels of cells without an own tour onto a single shared depot tour, while the daily baseline paid a separate tour per cell and day. At partial adoption roughly a quarter of all parcels rode a tour that the baseline structurally could not have. With the universal tour rule of Section~\ref{sec:tour_rule} applied to both sides, the corner is flat: at $(10, 0.1)$ the routing-optimal plan saves \provisional{$0.03\%$} of routing cost and \provisional{$0.02\%$} of operator cost, and only \provisional{$2.9\%$} of cells remain non-daily, against $42\%$ in the submitted version. A symmetric re-computation of the submitted grid, in which both sides may pool and each depot-day takes the cheaper of pooled and unpooled service, lowers the baseline by $0.74\%$ and the headline at $(0, 1)$ from $22.79\%$ to $22.22\%$, which bounds the size of the effect at full adoption. What survives at partial adoption is genuine but modest, and it appears mainly in the operator lens: at $(0, 0.1)$ the routing-optimal plan saves \provisional{$1.4\%$} of routing cost, and the operator-polished plan \provisional{$3.9\%$} of operator cost.

\autoref{tab:two_lens} reports the headline outcomes at full adoption under both lenses and for both plans, and \autoref{fig:grid_heatmap_6} the corresponding surfaces over the full $(P, \theta)$ grid. Three findings stand out. First, in the routing lens the picture of the submitted version survives: the routing-optimal plan saves \provisional{$23.1\%$} at $(0, 1)$ and the saving decays monotonically with $P$. Second, in the operator lens that same plan is \provisional{$7.8\%$} \emph{worse} than daily delivery. Its two-day patterns concentrate each depot's work on a few weekdays, so the summed depot peak rises by \provisional{$34.5\%$}; under weekly staffing the vehicle-days it avoids below the peak were never a cash saving. Third, the operator-cost polish reverses this: it saves \provisional{$24.7\%$} of operator cost at $(0, 1)$ while cutting the summed depot peak by \provisional{$16.9\%$}, and it still realizes \provisional{$20.4\%$} in the routing lens --- it gives up \provisional{$2.7$}~pp of routing saving to buy the peak reduction. The polish is never negative in the operator lens anywhere on the grid, whereas the routing-optimal plan turns negative there under partial adoption, down to \provisional{$-10.4\%$} at $(0, 0.9)$. Partial adoption is thus worthwhile in the operator lens and close to neutral in the routing one.

Read as a pure cost objective, neither lens points to $P = 0.25$. At full adoption the saving decreases with $P$ in both, so on cost alone the routing lens prefers $P = 0$ by \provisional{$3.9$}~pp and the operator lens by \provisional{$1.9$}~pp. We nevertheless recommend $P = 0.25$~\euro/p/d, because that is the point at which the service side is priced in. At $(0.25, 1)$ the operator plan saves \provisional{$22.8\%$} of operator cost and \provisional{$17.1\%$} of routing cost, cuts the summed depot peak by \provisional{$17.1\%$} --- as deeply as the cost-optimal extreme does --- and halves the additional wait (\provisional{$0.39$} against \provisional{$0.77$}~d). That halving costs \provisional{$1.9$}~pp of operator saving, and Section~\ref{sec:results_discount} shows that once the delay is actually paid for it stops being a trade: under a flat discount $P = 0.25$ is simply the best point on the grid. On the fleet side, the Monday--Saturday coefficient of variation of the system fleet falls from \provisional{$0.139$} in the baseline to \provisional{$0.024$} at $(0.25, 1)$ and \provisional{$0.047$} at $(0.5, 1)$, that is by \provisional{$83$} and \provisional{$66\%$}, with a grid maximum of \provisional{$88\%$} at $(0, 0.7)$. The baseline value is \provisional{$0.139$} under the partition-aware vehicle accounting used here against $0.135$ in the submitted version, so coefficient-of-variation reductions must not be compared across grid versions. Stage~2 is a local search and the gap it leaves is small but real: at $(0, 1)$ its summed depot peak of \provisional{$1{,}030$} vehicles stands against a flat-profile lower bound of \provisional{$980$}, and at $(0.25, 1)$ the residual gap is \provisional{$32$} peak vehicles.

The mechanism behind the operator-lens result is depot structure rather than consolidation depth. In the operator lens, consolidation pays where a depot can \emph{rotate} delivery days across several cells, so that the cells' busy days fall on different weekdays and the depot's peak stays flat. A depot serving a single cell has no rotation available: under any two-day pattern its weekly profile is one large peak and one large trough, and its peak falls only when it delivers more often. Nine of DHL's sixteen depots serve exactly one cell, and the polish returns them to near-daily delivery, while DHL's largest depot, serving thirteen cells, keeps rotated two-day patterns. At one-cell depots, temporal consolidation therefore saves kilometres but not fleet.

\subsection{Paying for the delay: a discount scenario}
\label{sec:results_discount}

The service penalty $P$ steers the search but is not a cash flow. It is worth asking what remains if the operator actually pays customers for waiting. We evaluated two payout rules on the same grid, for both plans and both lenses: (a) $P$~\euro\ per parcel and waiting day, that is, the steering penalty turned into a real payment, and (b) a flat $0.50$~\euro\ per delayed parcel, independent of how long it waits. A parcel counts as delayed if it arrives on a non-delivery day of its cell and its recipient is willing to wait; at full adoption that is \provisional{$680$} thousand parcels per week at $P = 0$, falling to \provisional{$404$}, \provisional{$248$}, \provisional{$165$}, and \provisional{$111$} thousand at $P = 0.25$, $0.5$, $0.75$, and $1$, out of $1.26$~million.

Under rule (a) the operator plan retains between \provisional{$24.7$} and \provisional{$6.2\%$} of operator saving across those five penalties (\provisional{$20.4$} to \provisional{$1.0\%$} in the routing lens): paying the penalty out roughly halves the saving from $P = 0.25$ onwards, but never eliminates it. Under the flat rule (b) the ordering of the operating points changes. $P = 0$ is no longer best, because it delays too many parcels: the net operator saving is \provisional{$8.6\%$} at $P = 0$ against \provisional{$13.2\%$} at $P = 0.25$ and \provisional{$12.6\%$} at $P = 0.5$. A 50-cent discount thus moves the optimum to $P = 0.25$--$0.5$ and reinforces the recommendation above. The break-even discount --- the most an operator could pay per delayed parcel and still break even --- rises with $P$ from \provisional{$0.77$} to \provisional{$2.24$}~\euro\ in the operator lens and from \provisional{$0.57$} to \provisional{$1.24$}~\euro\ in the routing lens, so the schedule can absorb a discount well above the 50 cents assumed here. At partial adoption the discount decides which lens is positive: at $P = 0.25$ and $\theta < 1$ it carries the routing lens into the red (\provisional{$-0.3$} to \provisional{$-2.4\%$}) while the operator lens stays at \provisional{$+3$} to \provisional{$+6\%$}.

Two kinds of evidence bear on whether the surrogate can be trusted at the operating points reported above. The first is a direct comparison against solver-routed tours. Across $1{,}247$ cells that VROOM solved to completion, the surrogate's overall error is $3.03\%$ MAPE at a bias of $+2.73\%$, and the bias is positive throughout: $+1.75\%$ on cells delivered daily against $+2.51\%$ on cells delivered twice and $+3.51\%$ on cells delivered three times a week. The surrogate therefore overprices consolidated schedules more than daily ones, so the savings reported here are understated rather than overstated. Accuracy also improves rather than degrades as instances grow, which is what the hybrid architecture predicts: the Daganzo backbone is a continuum approximation and becomes asymptotically better with instance size, while the learned residual corrects only where the training pool is dense. The hybrid hands over to the physical model rather than extrapolating blindly.

The second is re-routing the optimized schedules themselves. In the previous revision, at $\theta = 1$ and four operating points $P \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75\}$, the surrogate matched the solver-actual system-wide saving within $0.9$--$2.7$~pp and was conservative at all four ($22.8\%$ predicted vs.\ $23.7\%$ actual at $P = 0$, $18.5$ vs.\ $19.8$, $13.5$ vs.\ $15.6$, and $10.2$ vs.\ $13.0$). Those points were produced before the universal tour rule and the operator polish; they are evidence on the surrogate, not a validation of the plans reported here. Re-validation of the revised pipeline is under way and reported in this section in the final version: both lenses, the four full-adoption points, and at least one partial-adoption point at which pooled tours actually occur --- the regime that no earlier validation covered, since at $\theta = 1$ no pooled tour exists at all --- together with the pricing error of the bundle head against its fallback (pending).

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Seven limitations and one methodological caveat bound these results. (i) The operator-lens outcome is shaped by depot structure, and one carrier dominates it: nine of DHL's sixteen depots serve a single cell, and DHL operates the only multi-depot network in the case study. The rotation mechanism is therefore evidenced within a single carrier's network, and neither its magnitude nor its generality can be established from these data. (ii) Pooling is strictly within a depot, since every tour starts and ends at the depot of its cells, so the model cannot exploit cooperation between neighbouring depots or between providers; lifting that restriction is the most promising direction for future work and would raise the achievable savings. (iii) Stage~2 is a local search, and we report its gap to the flat-profile bound rather than claiming optimality. (iv) All optimization is surrogate-based, and the solver-validated points available so far lie at $\theta = 1$, where no pooled tours exist, so the partial-adoption regime is not yet validated against the solver (Section~\ref{sec:results_validation}). (v) The bundle head is certified on \provisional{$44.0\%$} of the pooled instances that occur; elsewhere a pooled tour is charged as the sum of its members' single-cell prices, which is conservative but coarse, and the certification is specific to this region's cell geometry. (vi) Fleet accounting changed with the tour rule: the baseline Monday--Saturday coefficient of variation is \provisional{$0.139$} here against $0.135$ in the submitted version, so fleet-smoothness figures are comparable within a grid version but not across versions. (vii) The operator lens inherits the labour double count of the label cost model (Section~\ref{sec:lenses}): its absolute levels are high by roughly a fifth, while savings, which take the inflated term in baseline and scenario alike, are unaffected. The methodological caveat concerns feature construction: for six of the seven providers the per-stop demand array falls back to one parcel per demand point, so two of the $25$ features are populated differently at run time than during training. The deployed model was validated end-to-end in exactly this state ($3.03\%$ MAPE, $+2.73\%$ bias on $1{,}247$ solver-routed cells), so the reported numbers stand, but the skew costs accuracy that a re-trained model would recover.







\section{Conclusion}
\label{concl}

We presented an ML-based surrogate optimization framework for time-based consolidation in last-mile parcel delivery on a case study of $312$ provider--postal-code cells formed by seven LSPs over $48$ postal-code areas, with $1.26$~million parcels per week in the Hanover Region. The Daganzo-LightGBM hybrid surrogate achieved $2.95\%$ MAPE under postal-code-grouped cross-validation, and a universal minimum/maximum tour rule keeps every priced tour --- in the daily baseline as in every scenario --- inside the instance family the surrogate was trained on, which removes the pooling asymmetry that inflated the partial-adoption results of the submitted version.

The central result of this revision is that the answer depends on the cost lens. Under a routing lens that charges every vehicle-day in full, the cost-optimal plan saves \provisional{$23.1\%$} at full adoption. Under an operator lens, in which drivers are employed for the week and only the depot peak carries the fixed bill, the very same plan is \provisional{$7.8\%$} worse than daily delivery, because its two-day patterns raise the summed depot peak by \provisional{$34.5\%$}. Re-optimizing the second stage in the operator's own currency --- a frequency-free local search that accepts a move only if variable cost, peak cost, and waiting penalty jointly fall --- turns this into \provisional{$24.7\%$} of operator saving at a \provisional{$16.9\%$} lower peak fleet, for \provisional{$2.7$}~pp of forgone routing saving. Consolidation pays in the operator lens where a depot can rotate delivery days across several cells; where a depot serves a single area it returns to near-daily delivery, because there consolidation saves kilometres but no fleet.

On cost alone both lenses prefer the cost-optimal extreme, by \provisional{$3.9$}~pp in the routing lens and \provisional{$1.9$}~pp in the operator lens. We nevertheless recommend $P = 0.25$~\euro/p/d, which retains \provisional{$22.8\%$} of operator saving at half the additional wait and an equally deep peak reduction, and which a flat $0.50$~\euro\ discount per delayed parcel turns into the best point on the grid outright. Per-LSP chord-distance optima still sort the operators into service-bound, hybrid, and cost-aggressive classes, but the sorting is itself lens-dependent: three carriers move up one class under the operator lens. Where consolidation is worth having at all is set by depot structure and not by geography alone: a depot that can rotate delivery days across several cells consolidates, one that serves a single cell does not, and the spatial breakdown of savings is re-derived from the revised grid and reported with the final results. For practice this suggests differentiating the service by provider and depot rather than offering it uniformly, and --- decisively --- planning the weekly roster together with the schedule, because a plan that is cost-optimal per day can be loss-making per week. Validation relies on VROOM as a routing benchmark rather than on ground truth, and the partial-adoption regime with pooled tours is not yet solver-validated. Pooling also remains within a depot, so inter-depot and inter-provider cooperation, which could amplify the gains, is left to future work.
